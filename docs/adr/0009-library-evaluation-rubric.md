# 0009. Library evaluation rubric and ADR template

* Status: Accepted
* Date: 2026-09-19
* Issue: [PAP-209](https://linear.app/paperos/issue/PAP-209)
* Deciders: Scout (Library Evaluator); Atlas confirms weights and gates, Iris the a11y anchors, Sentinel the CLI (see Consequences → Outstanding sign-off)
* Review date: 2026-12-19

## Context

Twenty-odd Claude sessions run in parallel and each of them will, sooner or
later, ask "should we adopt X". Six research issues already cite a rubric that
did not exist: PAP-31 (local-first sync), PAP-56 (auth), PAP-127 (canvas and
editor), PAP-139 (CRDT), PAP-188 (CRM and marketing) and PAP-182. Fifteen more
(PAP-211 to PAP-216, PAP-292 to PAP-296, PAP-350, PAP-493, PAP-755, PAP-763)
consume its outputs.

Without one rubric the failure mode is predictable and expensive: every
character invents their own criteria, each ADR argues in a different currency,
Atlas cannot compare two recommendations, and a library gets adopted because
the session that looked at it liked it. Two further constraints are specific to
PaperOS:

- We ship inside **Tauri WebViews** (`webkit2gtk` on Linux, `WKWebView` on
  macOS/iOS), not Chromium. A UI library that only works in Chrome is not a
  trade-off, it is a non-starter.
- Most of the code is written by agents. A library that a Claude session gets
  wrong on the first try costs more than a library that is 20 KB heavier, so
  **agent-friendliness has to be a scored criterion**, not a footnote.

The ADR format is also unsettled: the build loop pre-assigned ADR numbers
0001-0025 and told sessions to use Nygard style until a template existed, while
PAP-130 will later add `pnpm adr new|lint|index` over a strict frontmatter
schema. Sessions are writing ADRs 0002-0008 right now, so the template has to
be compatible with what they are already writing, not a reformatting order.

## Decision

We adopt **one rubric** for every adoption question, recorded in
[`docs/platform/library-rubric.md`](../platform/library-rubric.md) with a
machine-readable copy at
[`packages/agents/src/rubric/library-rubric.json`](../../packages/agents/src/rubric/library-rubric.json),
and **one ADR template** at [`docs/adr/template.md`](template.md).

1. **Six criteria, weights summing to 100**: license 20, maintenance 20, bundle
   15, a11y 15, TypeScript quality 15, agent-friendliness 15. License and
   maintenance carry the most weight because they are the two that cannot be
   fixed by us later; the four 15s are deliberately equal so no character can
   argue their speciality outranks another's.
2. **0-4 anchors for every criterion**, written out so `1` and `3` are not
   invented per session. Score = `Σ (score/4 × weight)`, 0-100.
3. **`n/a` drops and rescales** the remaining weights to 100 rather than
   scoring 0 — a headless library is not inaccessible, it has no UI.
4. **Five hard gates**, evaluated before scoring, any failure forcing
   `reject` whatever the total: `licenseTier`, `noTypes`, `webviewIncompatible`,
   `vendorNoSelfHost`, `openAdvisory` (critical).
5. **Thresholds**: ≥ 75 `adopt`, 60-74 `trial` (behind one boundary or flag,
   with a `reviewDate` ≤ 90 days), < 60 `reject`.
6. **Evidence rule**: a score of 3 or 4 requires a URL or a repo path, or the
   CLI fails and names the criterion. Vendor claims are never facts; unknown
   facts are written `unknown` with how to find out.
7. **Ties within 5 points** are broken by `migrationCostHours`, then by the
   owning character's written judgement in the ADR. **Never by re-scoring.**
8. **Extras**: two standard optional extras, `security` (OpenSSF Scorecard,
   advisories) at weight 10 and `packaging` (`publint`, `arethetypeswrong`) at
   weight 5, plus at most two domain extras of 5 or 10 points each, capped at
   25 points total, applied identically to every candidate in a comparison.
9. **The license gate reads PAP-211's `ops/licenses/policy.yaml`** rather than
   duplicating tiers. Until that file exists the rubric JSON carries
   `licensePolicy.draftTiers`, the CLI prints `license policy: draft
   (PAP-211 unmerged)`, and the scorecard records
   `facts.licensePolicySource: draft` so those scorecards can be re-checked.
10. **The ADR template** carries PAP-130's frontmatter (`id`, `title`,
    `status`, `date`, `deciders`, `issue`, `supersedes`, `supersededBy`,
    `tags`, `reviewDate`) over a Nygard body — Status, Context, Decision,
    Consequences, Alternatives rejected — plus the two PaperOS additions the
    rubric needs: the **Alternatives table** (generated by `pnpm lib score`,
    pasted verbatim) and **Re-open criteria** (a date *and* a checkable fact).

## Consequences

**Positive.** Two ADRs from two characters are comparable at a glance. A library
cannot be adopted on taste: the gates kill the four failures we actually fear
(a license we cannot ship, an untyped package, something that dies in
`webkit2gtk`, a vendor with no self-host path) before the argument starts. The
machine-readable copy means the CLI, the registry check (PAP-216) and the
weekly scan (PAP-218) all read the same numbers as the prose. Agent-friendliness
being 15 % of the score makes "can a session use this correctly on the first
try" a thing we measure rather than a thing we complain about.

**Negative.** Scoring six criteria with evidence is real work per candidate —
roughly 20-40 minutes once facts are collected — which is why surveys are
time-boxed and partial ADRs are allowed. Weights are a blunt instrument: a
library can clear 75 while being weak on the one axis that matters for its
domain, which is what the domain extras and the written judgement in the ADR
exist to catch. Anchors will need tuning after the first half-dozen real
scorecards; that tuning is an ADR, not an edit.

**Neutral.** Scorecards live in `docs/libraries/scorecards/` as YAML, so they
are diffable and re-scorable but not queryable until the registry lands.
Existing ADRs 0001-0008 stay as written; the template does not require a
retrofit, only that new ADRs use it.

**Outstanding sign-off.** The spec asks for Atlas to approve weights and gates,
Iris to confirm the a11y anchors and Sentinel to pass the CLI. Atlas and Iris
are asked in comments on PAP-209; the CLI is not built in this session (see
*Gaps*), so Sentinel's pass is deferred to the follow-up issue.

## Alternatives rejected

**No rubric — each character argues their case in prose.** This is the status
quo and it is what the six research issues were about to do. It fails on
comparability: Atlas cannot rank two recommendations written in different
currencies, and nothing stops a session skipping the license question until CI
finds it. Rejected. Would change if the number of parallel sessions dropped to
about two, where a shared memory is cheaper than a shared form.

**A short checklist (pass/fail per criterion) instead of scores.** Cheaper and
faster, and it is what the hard gates already are. But most real decisions are
between two libraries that both pass everything, and a checklist has nothing to
say about "which one". Rejected as the whole answer, adopted as the gate layer.

**A longer rubric (10-12 criteria: community, docs, i18n, theming, testing,
performance, ...).** More faithful to how an evaluation actually feels, and
several of those matter a lot for specific domains. Rejected because every
criterion is paid for by twenty sessions on every candidate, and the long tail
is domain-specific — which is exactly what the two-extra allowance in §3 of the
rubric covers, at the cost of thinking per comparison rather than always.

**Per-project weights (tables weights bundle more, identity weights security
more).** Tempting and defensible. Rejected because it destroys the one property
we are buying: comparability. A per-project weight set means a score of 78 in
one project is not a score of 78 in another, and the first person to notice
will be the person whose library lost. Domain extras give the same expressive
power while keeping the base six fixed and visible.

**Adopt an off-the-shelf model (OpenSSF Scorecard alone, or the CNCF/Thoughtworks
radar style).** OpenSSF Scorecard is excellent and we consume it — as the
`security` extra. It says nothing about bundle size, accessibility, TypeScript
soundness or whether Claude knows the library, which are four of our six.
Rejected as a replacement, adopted as an input.

**Separate ADR templates for library decisions and architecture decisions.**
Would let the library template hard-require the Alternatives table. Rejected:
two templates drift, and sessions writing ADRs 0002-0008 right now would have to
pick. One template, with the table marked required for library adoptions, keeps
`pnpm adr lint` (PAP-130) simple.

| Option | Comparability | Cost per candidate | Catches our real failures | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Six criteria + 5 gates + extras** | high | medium | yes | **adopt** |
| No rubric (prose) | none | low | no | reject |
| Checklist only | medium | low | gates only | folded in as the gate layer |
| 10-12 criteria | high | high | yes | reject (cost) |
| Per-project weights | low | medium | yes | reject (defeats the purpose) |
| OpenSSF Scorecard alone | medium | low | security only | folded in as the `security` extra |

## Re-open criteria

- **Date.** `reviewDate` 2026-12-19, after the first wave of real scorecards
  (PAP-212 to PAP-216, PAP-292 to PAP-296, PAP-350).
- **Fact — anchors misfire.** Three or more scorecards land where the verdict
  from the total disagrees with the owning character's written recommendation.
  That is the anchors being wrong, and it is a rubric change.
- **Fact — a gate never fires or always fires.** After 15 scorecards, a gate
  with zero failures or one that has been waived more than twice is not a gate.
- **Fact — the license policy changes shape.** If PAP-211 lands tiers that do
  not map onto the `license` anchors (for example per-context scoring rather
  than per-context tiering), the criterion is rewritten.
- **Budget.** If median scoring time per candidate exceeds 60 minutes across a
  survey, the rubric is too heavy and criteria get merged.
